Estimate the failure rate of an electronic board from its parts list and mission profile, following the IEC TR 62380:2004 approach as described in public sources, with MIL-HDBK-217F models for the parts it does not cover.

The IEC TR 62380 coefficients are not taken from the standard. Most were copied from one open-source project and have not been checked against IEC TR 62380. Parts marked MIL-HDBK-217F use that public-domain handbook, a different and older method. Treat results as rough estimates: they are not suitable as evidence for an ISO 26262 or IEC 61508 safety case. Every table value can be overridden per part. See where each table came from.

Mission profile

One row per phase of a typical year. tac is the air temperature around the board. τ is the fraction of the year the equipment works in that phase (0 for a dormant phase). n and ΔT describe the thermal cycles the board goes through. Tick “+ΔT/3” where each cycle also heats the part by a third of its own temperature rise (the “ΔTj/3 +” entries in the standard). Rows copied from a spreadsheet can be pasted into the table.

    Circuit board

    The bare board’s main wear-out: plated holes and vias crack around the barrel because the laminate swells through its thickness far more than copper does, much more once it passes its glass transition (Tg) in reflow. Following the IPC-TR-579 model as published in open papers (see Data sources), each hole size gets a cycles-to-failure life from the board thickness, drill, plating and laminate; the assembly reflows use up part of that life and the mission profile’s thermal cycles the rest, and the weakest hole size adds its rate, averaged over the mission length, to the board total. The plating’s quality KQ and strain distribution Kd move the result a lot, so they are entered rather than assumed.

    Surface-mount solder joints

    For surface-mount boards: thermal cycling slowly cracks solder joints, worst on large leadless and ceramic parts. Following MIL-HDBK-217F section 16.2, each device listed here gets a cycles-to-failure life from its size, joint height, leads and body against the board's expansion, and the worst one (the weakest link) adds its rate, averaged over the mission length, to the board total. The model was derived for tin-lead solder and leaves out manufacturing defects. IEC TR 62380 parts already include joint fatigue in their package terms, so this counts it again for them; untick the box to leave it out.

    Electrolytic capacitor life

    Aluminium electrolytic capacitors wear out: a wet one's electrolyte slowly dries, a polymer one's polymer degrades. Each line is worked out from its rated life with the makers' published rules: wet and hybrid capacitors last twice as long for every 10 °C cooler, polymer ones ten times as long for every 20 °C, and ripple heating shortens the life. Below 40 °C the life is worked out at 40 °C, and no life is taken as longer than 15 years. End of life is a drift limit (capacitance down, ESR up), so it counts as a failure only where drift matters in the circuit: filters, regulator outputs (an LDO can go unstable outside its ESR range), timing and hold-up. Where only shorts and opens matter, such as bulk storage with margin, the life is still checked and you are warned if it is shorter than the mission. Wear-out times spread around the calculated life as Cornell Dubilier publishes (mean 1.2 ×, deviation 0.1 ×), so a capacitor that outlasts the mission adds almost nothing, and the share worn out by the end of the mission is counted as an average rate over it.

    Reliability structure

    Every part starts in “Everything else, in series”: any failure fails the board. Put parts that back each other up into a parallel block, such as capacitors on a rail, then say how many must keep working and what share of their failures breaks the block anyway (a shorted capacitor takes the whole rail down). Blocks nest, so two series power paths can sit inside a 1-of-2 block. The tree and the diagram edit the same structure. Netlist import proposes blocks of parts wired to the same nets; they count in series until you accept them. A redundant block has no constant failure rate, so the board figures become averages over the mission length.
